# Weekly Self-Review Block

The maintaining practice for **CI of Self**.

> Your discernment, your AI literacy, your judgment about when to trust output and when to
> overrule it.

Thirty to sixty minutes once a week, spent reviewing your own AI-assisted output specifically
for discernment failures. Places you accepted an output you should have challenged. Places you
over-prompted instead of thinking first. The output of the block is not a deliverable. It is
the maintenance of your own ability to read your work honestly.

---

## Before the first one

**Put it on the calendar as a recurring event.** The slot matters more than the length. A
consistent forty-five minutes beats an occasional ninety, because the practice depends on
comparing this week to last week, and an irregular block has nothing to compare against.

**Pick the slot deliberately.** Late in the week works for most people, because the artifacts
are fresh and you are past the point of wanting to improve them. Monday morning tends to fail:
the work is a week old and the week ahead is pulling.

**Decide now what you will do when it collides with something.** It will, in week three. The
answer that works is "move it, do not skip it." The answer that does not work is deciding in
the moment.

## Inputs

Three AI-assisted outputs you shipped this week.

**Do not pick your best three.** This is the single most important rule in the kit and the one
most often broken quietly. Picking the ones you are proud of turns the block into a review of
your highlights, which measures nothing. If you cannot bring yourself to choose badly, choose
randomly: the three most recent, or three by a coin flip.

"Shipped" means someone else saw it. Drafts do not count, because the thing being examined is
what you were willing to put your name on.

## The block: four passes, 45 minutes

| Pass | Time | What you do | What you write |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cold read | 10 min | Reread each artifact **without opening the chat that produced it** | Per artifact: "Could I defend every claim in this right now, without the transcript?" Yes or no, plus the first claim you could not. |
| 2. The accept log | 15 min | For each place you took model output as-is | Three columns: what I accepted / what I would have written myself / why I did not write it |
| 3. Over-prompt check | 10 min | Count the prompts per artifact | For the highest count: the one sentence of thinking you were avoiding by prompting again |
| 4. One commitment | 10 min | Pick exactly one behaviour to change | Written as a trigger, not an intention |

### Pass 1: cold read

Closing the transcript is the whole mechanism. With the chat open you reconstruct your
reasoning from the conversation and it feels like memory. Without it you find out what you
actually retained, which is the thing being measured.

### Pass 2: the accept log

This is the pass that does the work, and it is the uncomfortable one. The third column is
where the finding lives. "Why I did not write it myself" has a small number of honest answers,
and they are very different from each other:

- It was better than what I would have written. (Fine. Note it and move on.)
- It was faster and the quality difference did not matter here. (Fine, and worth trusting.)
- I did not know enough to write it. (A gap. Is it one you want to keep?)
- I did not want to think about it right then. (The one to watch.)

Nobody needs to see this log. Its value is entirely a function of how honestly it is written,
and it stops working the moment it has an audience.

### Pass 3: over-prompt check

Count the prompts. Take the artifact with the highest count. Write the sentence of thinking
you were avoiding.

The count in isolation means nothing; eleven prompts on a hard problem is fine. The trend over
eight weeks is the signal, which is why the worksheet has a tracker and why a single block
cannot tell you much.

### Pass 4: one commitment

**Exactly one.** Written as a trigger:

> When *(situation)*, I will *(action)* before prompting.

A trigger fires on a condition you will actually encounter. An intention ("be more careful",
"think first") does not fire on anything and will not survive the week.

Good: "When I am about to send a third prompt on the same paragraph, I will close the tab and
write the next sentence by hand."

Not good: "I will over-prompt less."

## What done looks like

- Three artifacts reviewed, and not your best three.
- At least one accept-log row you are not comfortable with.
- One trigger commitment, in your own words.
- The block ended when the timer ended.

## What done is not

- A better prompt.
- A longer or improved artifact.
- A to-do list.

If the block produced a deliverable, it did not happen. That is failure mode 3, and it is the
most common way this practice dies. Read the definition of done before you start, not after.

## Anti-patterns

**The block becomes a production block.** You have an hour of protected focus and the week is
demanding work from you. Watch for it around week four.

**Reviewing only the work you are proud of.** Covered above, and worth restating because it
happens silently.

**Writing commitments as intentions.** If your commitment has no "when", rewrite it.

**Skipping pass 2.** It is the uncomfortable one. It is also the point of the exercise.

## The decay tracker

Eight weeks, on the worksheet. Columns: week, block held, artifacts reviewed, accept-log rows,
**last week's trigger kept**.

The last column is the real metric. Blocks held is attendance. Triggers kept is practice. It
is entirely possible to hold eight blocks in a row and change nothing, and the tracker is
designed so that you cannot avoid noticing if that is what happened.
